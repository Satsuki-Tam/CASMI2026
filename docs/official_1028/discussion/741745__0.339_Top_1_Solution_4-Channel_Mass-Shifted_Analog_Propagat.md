1. My Current View: The Spectrum Retrieval Problem Has Multiple Tiers

One of the main observations from my experiments is that the 400 test molecules do not appear to behave like a single homogeneous retrieval problem.

My current working hypothesis is that they can roughly be divided into three groups:

Cohort
Rough Share
Description
Useful Strategy

Tier 1 — Library Match
~10–15%
Spectrum / precursor can be matched directly in available reference libraries
Exact spectral matching

Tier 2 — Database Known
~45–55%
The exact spectrum is new, but related structures can be found in natural-product databases
Analog propagation + neural reranking

Tier 3 — Novel / De Novo
~30–40%
Structural isomer or scaffold is difficult to recover from existing databases
Substructure reasoning + in-silico fragmentation

This was important for me because a pipeline relying only on train.parquet quickly reached a plateau around the ~0.15 region.

That led me to focus much more heavily on candidate retrieval outside the training set, followed by multi-stage reranking.

The percentages above are only my current estimates, not ground truth, so I would be very interested to hear whether others are seeing a different distribution.

Channel 1 — Exact Library Matching

The first stage is still conventional spectral retrieval:

- precursor mass filtering

- spectral entropy similarity

- cosine similarity

- reference peak matching

When the correct compound is already represented in a reference library, this channel is extremely strong.

Channel 2 — Mass-Shifted Analog Propagation

This was one of the biggest improvements for me.

Instead of asking only:

  “Do I have the exact spectrum?”

I also ask:

  “Do I have related molecules whose spectra can provide structural evidence?”

For Tier 2 candidates, I observed many cases where related natural products differ by relatively interpretable transformations such as:

- ±CH₂

- ±OH

- ±Hexose

- other common modifications

I therefore search for related spectra within a mass-shift window and propagate structural information through molecular similarity.

One of the scores I use is:

$$
\text{AnalogScore}(c)
=
\sum_{a \in \text{Analogs}}
\text{Sim}(q,a)^4
\cdot
\text{Tanimoto}(f_c,f_a)
$$

where:

- q = query spectrum

- a = analogous reference spectrum

- Sim(q, a) = spectral similarity between the query and the analog

- f_c = molecular fingerprint of candidate c

- f_a = molecular fingerprint of analog a

- Tanimoto(f_c, f_a) = fingerprint similarity between the candidate and the analog

The intuition is that good spectral analogs should provide useful structural priors even when the exact molecule is absent.

3. Channel 3 — Chemical Heuristics / MetFrag-Lite

I also added several explainable chemical checks to remove candidates that look good from a purely statistical perspective but are chemically implausible.

The current version includes:

- approximate bond dissociation reasoning

- neutral-loss matching

- substructure consistency

- simple in-silico fragmentation rules

This channel is not intended to completely solve structure elucidation.

Instead, it mainly acts as a candidate filter / prior before the final ranking stage.

4. Channel 4 — Spectrum-to-Fingerprint Transformer

This turned out to be one of the most interesting additions in my pipeline.

I trained a small 6-layer Transformer, which I call FPNet, to predict logits:

$$
z \in \mathbb{R}^{6930}
$$

corresponding to 6,930 fingerprint bits.

The model therefore learns:

$$
\text{MS/MS Spectrum}
\rightarrow
\text{Fingerprint Distribution}
$$

Rather than directly generating a molecule, I use the predicted fingerprint representation as another source of evidence for candidate ranking.

5. Why the Neural Score Can Be Extremely Fast

Assuming independent Bernoulli fingerprint bits, the log-likelihood of a candidate fingerprint $f_c$ can be written as:

$$
\log P(f_c \mid S)
=
f_c \cdot z
+
\sum_i \log \sigma(-z_i)
$$

For a fixed query spectrum, the second term is constant across all candidates.

Therefore:

$$
\text{Score}_{\text{neural}}(c)
=
f_c \cdot z
$$

This is useful because the entire candidate set can then be scored using a vectorized matrix multiplication.

In practice, this makes it cheap to evaluate tens of thousands of candidates after retrieval.

6. Final Ranking

I combine the different signals into a ranking matrix with 31 features, including information from:

- exact spectral similarity

- analog relationships

- precursor mass differences

- molecular similarity

- chemical heuristics

- Transformer fingerprint scores

- other retrieval statistics

I then use a HistGradientBoosting ensemble with:

- 4 seeds: (0, 1, 2, 3)

- 2 calibrated prior settings

followed by a confidence-based gating stage.

The final system produces the ranked Top-25 candidate SMILES used for submission.

7. What Did NOT Work

A few approaches looked promising initially but ended up making the ranking worse.

Blind PubChem isomer expansion

Expanding the candidate space too aggressively introduced a large number of synthetic / irrelevant decoys.

In my experiments, this hurt the ranking of the more difficult classes.

Curated natural-product databases such as COCONUT 2.0, ChEBI and LipidMaps were much more useful for my particular pipeline.

Overly Wide Mass Windows

I also experimented with wider precursor mass tolerances.

My current results suggest that a very wide window adds too many decoys, while a tighter window around ±8.5 ppm provided a better precision/recall trade-off for the data I was using.

Again, this is an empirical observation from my experiments rather than a universal rule.

8. What I Am Still Trying to Figure Out

Even at 0.339, I don't think the problem is solved.

The biggest open question for me is Tier 3:

  How can I recover truly novel structures when neither the exact molecule nor a useful close analog exists in the reference databases?

Some directions I am currently interested in include:

- better in-silico fragmentation

- substructure assembly

- graph-based candidate generation

- stronger spectrum-to-structure models

- contrastive learning between spectra and molecular fingerprints

- better uncertainty estimation for dynamic channel weighting

- retrieval models specifically trained for structural analogs
