Key Question 1 — Exact tasks, data and scoring of each edition, and how many challenge compounds the best methods identified at rank 1 / top-k

Takeaway

Every CASMI edition from 2012 to 2017 scored the absolute (worst-case) rank of the correct structure, matched on the first InChIKey block (connectivity, stereo ignored), and declared winners by per-challenge "wins" (later "gold medals"), with Top-1/Top-3/Top-10, median rank and relative ranking position (RRP) as secondary statistics; CASMI 2022 switched to single-answer match/no-match scoring across four tasks (adduct, formula, class, 2D structure). Best fully automatic top-1 structure rates for compounds without usable reference spectra plateaued around 30–35 % (2016 Cat 2: 70/208; 2017 Cat 2: 77/243; 2017 Cat 4: 66/198; 2022: 26–29 %), and only rose to ~70 % when database/metadata "boosting" was allowed (2016 Cat 3: 146/208).

Cited Findings

CASMI 2012 (contest Sept 2012–early 2013; results published 2013)

  • Organised and initiated by Emma Schymanski and Steffen Neumann; four categories: Cat 1 best molecular formula (LC-HRMS/MS), Cat 2 best structure (LC-HRMS/MS), Cat 3 formula (GC-MS, unit mass), Cat 4 structure (GC-MS). Target was 20 challenges per category, not reached: 14 LC-HRMS challenges (numbered 1–6 and 10–17) and 16 GC-MS challenges — Schymanski & Neumann 2013, "CASMI: Challenges and Solutions", Metabolites (PMC3901296)
  • LC challenges 1–6 came from plant material measured on a Bruker micrOTOF-Q (ESI positive, ≤5 ppm, 12,000 resolution); 10–17 were reference standards measured with ESI or APCI, CID or HCD; GC challenges came from environmental samples (3–16 from a Bitterfeld groundwater sample), all confirmed with reference standards. Challenge 2 raw data had ~30 ppm mass error and was recalibrated only after the contest closed — PMC3901296
  • No external submissions were received for the GC-MS categories 3–4 (only an internal entry shown "for completeness") — Schymanski & Neumann 2013, "CASMI: And the Winner is…", Metabolites (PMC3901266)
  • Scoring: absolute rank = number of candidates scoring better plus those scoring equal (worst case); per-challenge winner = best absolute rank; overall winner = most challenge wins; structures compared by the first InChIKey block (stereochemistry ignored); supplementary measures: relative ranking position (RRP), normalised score, weighted RRP and Tanimoto similarity of the top candidate — PMC3901266
  • Cat 1 results (correct/submitted; wins; avg rank): Dunn et al. (Birmingham, PUTMEDID-LCMS) 9/11, 9 wins, avg rank 1.11 → winner; Shen et al. (FingerID, ML) 8/14, 3 wins, 2.88; Dührkop et al. (SIRIUS) 8/14, 5 wins, 2.25; internal Neumann (Rdisop isotope script) 9/13; internal Meringer (MOLGEN-MS/MS) 11/14 — PMC3901266
  • Cat 2 results: Herbert Oberacher (automated searches of MassBank, METLIN, NIST, MSforID spectral libraries) 3/5 correct, all ranked first → winner; Dunn et al. (KEGG, ChemSpider, MetFrag + manual review) 3/11, avg rank 5.7; Shen et al. (FingerID → KEGG) 4/14, avg rank 5.5; internal: Meringer (MOLGEN structure generation) 5/6 avg rank 23.4; Gerlich (MetFusion) 11/14 avg rank 237.5; Ruttkies (MetFrag) 9/14 avg rank 401 — PMC3901266
  • Retrospective summary (2020): "the 2012 winner placed 36 % in the top 5 and 21 % in the top position" (i.e., 3/14) — McEachran et al. 2020, "Revisiting Five Years of CASMI Contests with EPA Identification Tools", Metabolites (PMC7345619)
  • Example of in silico difficulty: for challenge 15, MetFrag ranked the correct answer 316 of 2585 PubChem candidates; MOLGEN-MS/MS Cat 2 absolute ranks were 63/171, 3/8, 3/4, 22/41, 26/32, 58/1295 — Meringer & Schymanski 2013, Metabolites (PMC3901272)

CASMI 2013 (challenges released 2 Sept 2013; winners declared 3 Feb 2014)

  • Organised by ten Japanese researchers (Nishioka, Kasama, Kinumi, Makabe, Matsuda, Miura, Miyashita, Nakamura, Tanaka, Yamamoto), sponsored by the Spectral Data Division of the Mass Spectrometry Society of Japan; 16 challenges, Cat 1 (formula) on 12 of them (formulas were given for 7, 8, 13, 14 because MS1 was unavailable), Cat 2 (structure) on all 16; masses 210 Da (dihydrochalcone) to 1,442 Da (cinnamtannin A3, a catechin pentamer); compounds = metabolites, agrochemicals (isoprothiolane, chlorpyrifos), environmental chemicals (2-(perfluorooctyl)ethanol), peptides, drugs (ofloxacin, aloxistatin), phospholipid; mostly ESI low-energy CID, challenge 13 MALDI-QIT, challenge 16 Orbitrap + QqQ; spectra deposited as MassBank MSJ00001–MSJ00034 — Nishioka et al. 2014, Mass Spectrometry (Tokyo) 3:S0039 (PMC4337369)
  • Scoring: rank via "rankWorstCase" (candidates scored better than or equal to the correct one); best rank wins the challenge, ties all win; most wins = overall title; organisers added "highly commended" structures judged likely to give near-identical spectra — PMC4337369; casmi-contest.org/2013/results
  • Official results page: Cat 1 winner Newsome & Nikolić (UIC, manual) 12/12 ranked first; Dührkop & Böcker (SIRIUS) 10/12; other automatic teams 8/8 (MAGMa) and 4/12 (CFM). Cat 2 winner Newsome & Nikolić 14/15 ranked first plus 1 commended ("15(1)/15"); Sweeney (MathSpec FragSearch) 8(1)/11 automatic + 2/3 manual; other automatic teams 7(1)/12, 7(1)/16 and 2/12 — casmi-contest.org/2013/results
  • Nishioka et al. table of challenge wins: Newsome & Nikolić 12/12 and 15/15; Ridder & van der Hooft (MAGMa) 8/8 and 7/12; Dührkop & Böcker (SIRIUS, Cat 1 only) 10/12; Schymanski & Neumann (MOLGEN-MS/MS; MetFrag/MetFusion) 5/12 and 8/16; Allen & Greiner (CFM) 4/8 and 2/12; Sweeney 9/14 (text says 7/11 — internal inconsistency); Miyazaki & Horai (manual) 0/10. "Best automated tool": MAGMa (8 of 9 organisers' votes), SIRIUS for Cat 1 — PMC4337369
  • Retrospective: "2013 winner placed 12 of 16 (75 %) in the top position" — PMC7345619

CASMI 2014 (challenges released 15 Sept 2014; deadline extended to 15 Feb 2015; results 18 Mar 2015)

  • 48 challenges in four blocks: 1–15 molecules detected in mammalian biofluids/tissues; 16–31 natural products of plant, fungal or bacterial origin; 32–42 synthetic/semi-synthetic molecules with human use (pharmaceuticals, pesticides, flame retardants, plasticisers); 43–48 "unknown compounds" defined as no hits in a SciFinder structure search, being unreported natural products or semi-synthetic derivatives of natural products. Two categories: Cat 1 best molecular formula, Cat 2 best structure, both allowing "additional information (sample background, experimental factors, retention information in some cases)"; participants had to declare automated vs manual — casmi-contest.org/2014/rules
  • Scoring: absolute rank with worst-case tie handling; winner per challenge; overall winner by number of victories; plus "score-based ranks" (normalised scores summed until the correct answer) to test usefulness of scores — casmi-contest.org/2014/rules
  • Instruments (per-challenge descriptions): Waters SYNAPT QTOF (positive ESI, C18, ~5–10 ppm), Thermo Orbitrap Velos and Q Exactive (HILIC amide and C18, HCD, NCE 25–90 %, <5 ppm), Shimadzu LC-MS-IT-TOF (negative ESI); retention times and sample matrix (serum, urine, CSF, plasma, cardiac tissue, plant Brassicaceae) were given — casmi-contest.org/2014/challenges
  • Overview paper: 7 teams from Europe, North America and Japan; 42 known and 6 unknown compounds; three teams solved 34/42 formulas; best team 24/42 structures; "formula determination is largely reliable, structure elucidation is still challenging, particularly for true unknowns" — Nikolić, Jones, Sumner, Dunn 2017, "CASMI 2014: Challenges, Solutions and Results", Current Metabolomics 5(1)
  • Official results: Cat 1 three-way tie at 34/42 (Vaniya, Allen, Ridder) → winner Lars Ridder (MAGMa) by tie-break (two more correct at rank 2); commendation to Tomáš Pluskal (MZmine) for 38/42 formulas within ranks 1–12; post-contest including unknowns 43–48: Ridder 40/48, Allen 38/48, Vaniya 36/48. Cat 2 winner Felicity Allen (CFM-ID) with 29 challenge wins and 24/42 structures ranked first; "only Lars Ridder and Felicity Allen had a hit among the unknown — challenge 47, ranked 2nd and 6th respectively" — casmi-contest.org/2014/results
  • MAGMa entry: correct formula ranked first in 40/48 challenges; correct structure ranked first in 22/42, in top 10 for 9 more; candidates from HMDB first, PubChem added when HMDB failed, large PubChem lists filtered by "refscore" — Ridder et al. 2017, "MAGMa-Based Mass Spectrum Annotation in CASMI 2014", Current Metabolomics; casmi-contest.org/2014/results-cat2
  • CFM-ID winning entry: 24/42 correct at rank 1 and 33 in the top 10 — Allen et al. 2017, Current Metabolomics (eurekaselect 76681); retrospective: "winner placed 30 in the top 5 and 24 in the top position" — PMC7345619
  • Per-challenge table for the unknowns 43–48 (Cat 2): every cell is "-" (correct structure absent) except challenge 46 (isomer 2) with ranks 6 and 2 — casmi-contest.org/2014/results-cat2 (note: the results summary text attributes the hit to challenge 47; the per-challenge table shows it under "challenge46(isomer2)" — numbering inconsistency on the site)

CASMI 2016 (data Dec 2015–Feb 2016; deadline 11/15 Apr 2016; results 6 May 2016)

  • Organised by Grégory Genta-Jouve (Paris Descartes), Olivier P. Thomas (Nice Sophia Antipolis), David Touboul (ICSN CNRS), Emma Schymanski (Eawag) and Steffen Neumann (IPB Halle), supported by RFMF; Schymanski and Neumann joined 25 Jan 2016 and the automatic Categories 2 and 3 plus 10 extra Cat 1 challenges were released 12 Feb 2016; prize: a bottle of wine per category; max three submissions per approach — casmi-contest.org/2016/index; 2016/rules
  • Category 1 (natural products, any method incl. manual): challenges 1–4 on an Agilent 6540 QTOF (<5 ppm, 20,000 res.), 5–9 on a Waters Synapt G2i QTOF (<10 ppm), 10–19 on a Q Exactive Plus Orbitrap (subset of Cat 2/3 data; 10–14 positive [M+H]+, 15–19 negative [M−H]−); challenge 3 removed (MS/MS of wrong precursor) → 18 scored — casmi-contest.org/2016/challenges-cat1; 2016/results-cat1
  • Cat 1 compounds: from plants, fungi, marine sponges and algae; e.g., bromopyrrole alkaloids (oroidin, dibromophakellin, ugibohlin), cytochalasins, cymoside, resin acids (abietic/neoabietic acid), anthraquinones (alizarin, purpurin), thyroxine, monensin; some compounds appeared twice from different instruments — Kind & Fiehn 2017, Phytochem Lett (PMC5720153); Nikolić 2017, Phytochem Lett (PMC5613954)
  • Cat 1 results (gold = challenge wins; Top1/Top3/Top10 of 18): Nikolić 15 gold, 14/18/18 (mean rank 1.22) → winner; Vaniya 14 gold, 14/15/15; Kind 12 gold, 12/14/15; Nothias-Scaglia (GNPS + CSI:FingerID) 11 gold, 11/11/12; Allen (CFM-ID) 7 gold, 7/12/16; Bertrand 6 gold, 6/8/12; Allard 2 gold, 2/6/10 — casmi-contest.org/2016/results-cat1
  • Categories 2 and 3 (automatic): all MS/MS on a Q Exactive Plus Orbitrap, <5 ppm, MS/MS resolution 35,000, stepped HCD 20/35/50, DDA with inclusion lists, C18 column; peak lists extracted with RMassBank and recalibrated to 5 ppm (peaks without a valid subformula removed). Training set: 312 peak lists from 285 substances (website says 234 positive + 58 negative, the paper says 254 positive + 58 negative; 254 is arithmetically consistent). Challenge set: 208 peak lists from 188 substances, 127 positive [M+H]+ and 81 negative [M−H]−; candidate lists retrieved from ChemSpider on 14 Feb 2016 as SMILES, converted with OpenBabel; RTs provided — casmi-contest.org/2016/challenges-cat2+3; Schymanski et al. 2017, J Cheminform 9:22 (PMC5368104)
  • Compounds "predominantly well-known anthropogenic substances" measured as 22 mixes of authentic standards (10–94 compounds each); spectra with MassBank match > 0.85 (MetFusion) went to training; candidate counts 1 to > 8000 per challenge; MS1 was not initially provided (added Dec 2016) — PMC5368104
  • Cat 2 rules: in silico fragmentation only, no spectral library search or metadata; Cat 3: same data and candidates, any metadata (RT, libraries, patents, reference counts) — PMC5368104
  • Scoring: ties get the average rank, stereoisomers collapsed by first InChIKey block keeping the best score; gold/silver/bronze per challenge; winner = most gold; Top1/3/10 with non-integer ranks rounded up; "Formula 1" points (25,18,15,12,10,8,6,4,2,1 for ranks 1–10); medal score 5/3/1; RRP — PMC5368104
  • Cat 2 results (gold; Top1/Top3/Top10 of 208; gold neg/pos): Brouard CSI:IOKR 86 gold; 62/93/118; 20/66 → winner; Dührkop CSI:FingerID 82 gold; 70/90/100; 0/82 (positive mode only); Vaniya MS-FINDER 1.62 70 gold; 46/79/101; 33/37; Allen CFM-ID 63 gold; 39/77/123; 26/37; Verdegem MAGMa+ 44 gold; 24/59/105; 24/20 — casmi-contest.org/2016/results-cat2. Organisers: "Category 2 results were very sensitive to the method of declaring the winner" — 2016/results
  • Cat 3 results: Kind (MS-FINDER + metadata "MD") 159 gold; Top1/3/10 = 146/162/174; Allen (CFM-ID retrained + database score) 156 gold; 120/160/182 — casmi-contest.org/2016/results-cat3; paper: "best combined entries reached ~163–164 Top 1 and 194–196 Top 10" — PMC5368104
  • Retrospective: 2016 challenge-set winner 169 in top 5 and 146 at top — PMC7345619

CASMI 2017 (rules public 26 Apr 2017; preliminary leaderboard 23 Aug; results 31 Oct 2017)

  • Organised by Dejan Nikolić (UIC), Nir Shahaf (Weizmann Institute), Emma Schymanski (Eawag), Steffen Neumann (IPB Halle); "fifth round"; focus on natural products; 45 challenges for Cat 1, up to 243 for Cats 2–3; two-stage submission procedure "inspired by the DREAM challenges" with a pseudonymised interim leaderboard — casmi-contest.org/2017/index; 2017/rules
  • Challenges 1–45 (UIC): "most are plant-derived compounds and a few endogenous to humans… some may be charged in their natural state"; a few "unknown" compounds with no PubChem/SciFinder hits (as of 03/2017) that are "unreported natural products or synthetic analogs of known natural products… to test the ability of current approaches to identify structural analogs of known natural products" (challenges 42–45 labelled "NP or synthetic analog"); Waters SYNAPT QTOF, ESI, ≤10 ppm MS and ~10 ppm/2 mDa MS/MS; MS1 + MS/MS + RT + chromatographic method + collision energy (6–30 eV or 6–50 eV ramp) and short origin descriptions provided; 33 positive / 12 negative; challenge 15 excluded after a late data fix — casmi-contest.org/2017/challenges_1-45
  • Challenges 46–243 (Weizmann): "all natural products", 46–131 negative mode (86), 132–243 positive mode (112); Waters HDMS SYNAPT QTOF in MS^E mode (low-energy 4 eV, high-energy ramp 10–30 eV pos / 15–35 eV neg), UPLC BEH C18 with PDA; <6 ppm; only MS/MS peak lists (no MS1), ion species not specified; Cat 4 candidate lists retrieved from PubChem (mirror 2017-02-03) with MetFrag 2.4.2 at 6 ppm assuming [M+H]+, [M+Na]+, [M+NH4]+ (pos) and [M−H]−, [M+Cl]−, [M+FA−H]− (neg), provided "full" (all stereoisomers) and "nonredundant" (one stereo per 2D) — casmi-contest.org/2017/challenges_46-243

CASMI 2022 (announced 5 Apr 2022; results due 6 Jun 2022; workshop at Metabolomics Society 2022, Valencia)

  • Organised by the team of Oliver Fiehn (UC Davis West Coast Metabolomics Center; contact Arpana Vaniya); framed as "comparative assessment of small molecule identifications… like an actual metabolomics study": raw LC-accurate-mass MS/MS data in +ESI and −ESI (mzML; .RAW or MSP on request), list of 500 compounds with retention times and precursor m/z; "compounds are not included in public libraries but comprise both metabolites and exposome chemicals"; Priority challenges 1–250, Bonus 251–500; challenges 81, 282, 432, 476 voided — casmi-contest.org/2022/index; fiehnlab.ucdavis.edu/casmi
  • Four evaluation categories: (1) adduct annotation, (2) elemental formula, (3) compound structure class, (4) 2D chemical structure (InChIKey or SMILES; only the first InChIKey block counted); single-answer submissions — "no candidate list is provided, so only the top-ranked answer counts"; wrong adduct in task 1 makes the dependent formula wrong; class judged manually with preference for higher-order classes; external and self-developed software allowed; multiple submissions per team allowed if different tools — fiehnlab.ucdavis.edu/casmi/casmi-faq; fiehnlab.ucdavis.edu/casmi
  • Acquisition detail disclosed: inclusion list, dynamic exclusion 0.8 s, no filters during mzML conversion; each challenge in one polarity only; bonus negative-mode data = 21 files covering 77 compounds; natural vs synthetic labels deliberately not provided — casmi-faq
  • 16 participating groups (Young/Toronto; Rutz/Geneva; Alcazar/UBC; Namini & Mosley/US EPA; Elser/Strasbourg; Nikolić/UIC; Wang/Alberta; Frigerio & Talavera Andujar/Luxembourg; Tsugawa/TUAT; Dührkop/Jena; Nothias/Geneva; Mehta & Bonini/oloBion; Xing/UBC; Wei/Nanjing; Ressom/OmicsCraft; Hong/Indiana). No winner declared; page states the trial "was aimed mostly to gauge which methods are used by the community, with which method or parameter variants, and how close we are" — fiehnlab.ucdavis.edu/casmi/casmi-2022-results

Key Question 2 — Which methods won each edition and why (fingerprint prediction + database retrieval, in silico fragmentation, spectral library search, ensembles, metadata)

Takeaway

Winners shifted from spectral-library search and expert manual work (2012–2013) to combinatorial in silico fragmentation with database priors (MAGMa, CFM-ID; 2013–2014), to machine-learned fingerprint prediction (CSI:FingerID/IOKR) whenever the task was spectrum-only (2016 Cat 2, 2017 Cats 1/2/4, and the top 2022 structure/formula/adduct scores), while rule-based MS-FINDER won whenever metadata and database-presence boosting was allowed (2016 Cat 3, 2017 Cat 3). Consensus of several in silico tools beat every individual tool in 2016, and manual expert dereplication still beat all automatic methods on small natural-product sets (2013, 2016 Cat 1).

  • The winning recipe when metadata is allowed has been constant since 2014: an in silico or ML spectrum–structure score plus a database-presence/popularity prior (HMDB membership, reference counts, data-source counts).   • Expert manual dereplication wins only when challenge counts are small (≤ 18–45), which is why organisers scaled to 208–500 challenges to make the contest meaningful for automatic methods; Kaggle's automatic code format completes that trajectory.

Key Question 3 — What organisers and participants concluded about candidate databases, isomer discrimination, instrument differences, and de novo identification

Takeaway

Across all editions the dominant conclusions were: (i) the candidate database is the single largest lever — restricting to a relevant curated database (HMDB, biomolecules, DSSTox) roughly doubles top-1 rates while risking total misses for compounds outside it, and popularity priors (reference/data-source counts) help for well-known anthropogenic compounds but hurt for natural products; (ii) MS/MS alone cannot resolve stereoisomers and often not positional isomers (1- vs 2-aminoanthraquinone 2012; abietic acid among 669 DNP isomers 2016; cytochalasins/resin acids 2016), so evaluation always collapses to InChIKey block 1; (iii) instrument/acquisition mismatches (mass-calibration errors, missing isotope patterns, integer-mass libraries, spectra differing between two instruments of the same type) are recurring failure modes; (iv) structures absent from databases were essentially never identified by any method.

Cited Findings

  • Candidate database (2012): "Smaller, specialised databases (KEGG) gave better ranks when they contained the answer, but missed more answers overall"; incomplete PubChem queries and wrong precursor masses caused missed candidates; a hard 5 ppm cutoff excluded correct formulas for SIRIUS and MOLGEN — PMC3901266
  • Candidate database (2016): "Restricting candidates (e.g., HMDB for human samples) helps. Reference counts and low IDs favored well-known substances, which may not generalize to emerging chemicals"; ChemSpider lists ranged from 1 to > 8000 candidates per challenge — PMC5368104; Blaženović: "without boosting, in silico tools identified only about 17–25 % of compounds correctly, so database and library information was essential for the known unknowns… Unknowns absent from databases remain the main challenge" — PMC5445034
  • Candidate database (retrospective 2020): "Database presence will therefore remain a biasing factor in NTA workflows for the foreseeable future"; curated DSSTox candidate sets outperformed broader sources like PubChem; in 2017, 13 solution InChIKeys had no DSSTox hits and 173 structures had to be registered before evaluation (only 54 of 243 were originally present); Data-Source-Count metadata reduced 2017 performance because many natural products had a single source; ~9 % (75) of all challenge compounds ranked outside the top 20, 65 of them from 2017 — PMC7345619
  • Candidate database size vs novelty (SIRIUS 4 re-evaluation): structure-disjoint CSI:FingerID on CASMI 2016 positive mode: 37.8 % against ChemSpider candidates vs 71.7 % against a ~0.5 M biomolecule database — Böcker lab blog
  • Natural-product database coverage (2016 Cat 1): cymoside was absent from DNP, CSLS and ChemSpider (present only in PubChem), so a team querying only DNP/Reaxys/CSLS submitted no correct candidate; "targeted natural product databases reduce isomer floods but can miss recently reported compounds" — PMC5720153
  • Isomers (2012): for challenge 10 all entries contained 2-aminoanthraquinone instead of 1-aminoanthraquinone (Tanimoto 0.842); MOLGEN authors: "positional isomers were hard to separate… MS/MS databases are often too small to judge isomers" — PMC3901266; PMC3901272
  • Isomers (2016): "Current MS/MS methods cannot distinguish stereoisomers. Positional isomers are a challenge for the next few years"; abietic acid had no Top-1 hit from any participant; monobenzyl phthalate's rearrangement was missed by combinatorial methods — PMC5368104; abietic acid had 669 DNP isomers and isopimaric acid's similar spectrum was ranked first (abietic acid 292nd); cytochalasin B core correct but stereo wrong in in silico tools — PMC5720153; "many isomers (cytochalasins, resin acids) gave near-identical spectra, so individual identifications had low confidence" — PMC5613954
  • Isomers/tautomers (retrospective): tautomers often failed to collapse under standard InChIKeys and stereochemistry was often missing or inconsistent in CASMI InChIKeys, complicating database matching — PMC7345619; the 2017 Cat 4 "nonredundant" candidate lists kept one arbitrary stereo configuration per 2D structure because "if your method gives identical scores to different stereoisomers, the pre-filtered candidates… result in the same evaluation" — casmi-contest.org/2017/rules-categories
  • Review view: LC-MS/MS alone "will often be unable to discriminate between stereoisomers and regioisomers, unless chiral columns are utilized"; ion mobility (CCS, RSD < 1–2 %) and RT proposed as orthogonal filters — Blaženović et al. 2018, Metabolites (PMC6027441)
  • Instrument/acquisition effects: 2012 ~30 ppm mass error (challenge 2) and non-standard ionisation (challenges 11, 12, 16) led to wrong precursor assignments — PMC3901266; 2013 challenge 13 MS2 turned out to be a mixture of two molecules; tools struggled with 210–300 Da compounds (236, 77 and 16 false positives above the answer for MetFrag/MetFusion, MAGMa, CFM on challenge 4) and with unclear mass accuracy/tautomers (challenges 11, 12) — PMC4337369; 2016: "integer-mass NIST 2014 data hurt CFM_retrain"; negative mode under-represented (816 negative training spectra) — PMC5368104; 2016 Cat 1: "spectra of the same compound differed between two instruments of the same type (dibromophakellin)", isotope ratios of dibromo compounds deviated from theory, and an isotopic-abundance error (19.48 % assumed as 10 %) produced a wrong formula — PMC5613954; PMC5720153; 2017: missing isotope patterns (MS^E data without MS1) caused SIRIUS to mis-rank formulas — Böcker lab blog; Blaženović recommends acquiring MS/MS at multiple collision energies because sparse spectra caused tool failures — PMC5445034
  • Training/test leakage: in CASMI 2016 "overlap with training data inflated results: Top 1 dropped from 34 % to 18 % when overlap was removed"; recommendations for future contests: "use true unknowns, assess positional isomers, provide open training sets that exclude challenge compounds, and require training-set InChIKey lists" — PMC5368104
  • Benchmarking good practice (2022): CASMI praised as "a notable endeavour to harmonise" tool comparison, but "the oftentimes small set of molecules used (i.e., few tens or hundreds)" is unlikely to generalise; 100-spectrum test sets have high variance; random draws from libraries inherit library biases (class, mass range, instrument, noise); "analysing the top 1 hit is the most informative approach" with top-n as complement; stratify by chemical class, mass, instrument; SIRIUS performance degrades above 500 Da and becomes computationally impractical above ~850 Da — de Jonge et al. 2022, Metabolomics (PMC9722809)
  • De novo / unknowns (2014): "No team correctly identified any of the unknown compounds" at rank 1; the only hit was ranked 2 and 6 — casmi-contest.org/2014/results; benthamdirect TOC
  • De novo (review 2018): "in silico fragmentation cannot elucidate unknown–unknown compounds, only known unknowns present in structure databases"; manual MS^n plus custom synthesis works but requires deep expertise; MINE-predicted compounds (> 500,000) proposed as hypotheses, with a warning that millions of in silico structures could cause "database poisoning" — PMC6027441
  • Scale of candidate space: PubChem and ChemSpider cover "almost 100 million compounds"; mass spectral databases contain about one million unique compounds — PMC6027441

Inferences

  • "98 % of database-route errors are same-formula isomers" (2026 context) is exactly the historical regime: once the formula is right, CASMI systems mostly failed by picking a wrong isomer; formula accuracy for the best systems was already 91–94 % (2016 SIRIUS 4 re-evaluation; 2022 Dührkop/Nothias/Rutz) and ~75 % for mid-tier tools.
  • Popularity priors (reference counts, data-source counts, low database IDs) are a double-edged inheritance: decisive in 2016 (anthropogenic standards) but harmful in 2017 (natural products). For a natural-product test set, a taxonomy/biosynthesis-aware prior (e.g., COCONUT membership, NP-likeness, organism co-occurrence as used by Bertrand's 2017 "Sphylo" score) is the historically supported replacement.
  • Instrument shift (Orbitrap/QTOF training → timsTOF test) was never explicitly benchmarked in CASMI, but every edition recorded failures from acquisition artefacts (calibration, missing isotopes, differing fragmentation), so robustness to peak-level perturbation and collision-energy variation is a transferable requirement.

Gaps

  • No CASMI edition reported a controlled cross-instrument experiment (same compounds on two vendors) with quantified performance loss; the only evidence is anecdotal (dibromophakellin 2016, two labs in 2017).
  • No source quantified how often CASMI 2016/2017 errors were same-formula isomers vs wrong formula; the 2026 "98 %" figure has no historical counterpart I could verify.

Key Question 4 — How CASMI 2014/2016/2017/2022 handled "novel" compounds not in databases, and what success rates were achieved

Takeaway

Only CASMI 2014 (challenges 43–48) and CASMI 2017 (challenges 42–45) deliberately included structures absent from PubChem/SciFinder (unreported natural products or synthetic analogues of known natural products); no participant identified any of them at rank 1 (2014: single hit at ranks 2/6; 2017 Cat 1: challenges 43 and 45 missed by every participant, 42 and 44 found only by CSI:FingerID at ranks 1 and 4). CASMI 2016 and 2022 instead used "known unknowns" — real compounds whose spectra were not in public libraries — and the best automatic top-1 on those was ~34 % (2016 Cat 2, spectrum only) to 70 % (2016 Cat 3, with metadata) and 26–29 % (2022, single answer, raw data).

Cited Findings

  • 2014 definition: "unknown compounds… the criterion for unknown is that a SciFinder search of the structure reveals no hits… the compounds included in this category will be unreported natural products or semi-synthetic derivatives of natural products (challenges 43–48)"; the winner was chosen on challenges 1–42 only — casmi-contest.org/2014/rules; 2014/results
  • 2014 outcome: "only Lars Ridder and Felicity Allen had a hit among the unknown… ranked 2nd and 6th"; Allen's workflow set DB_SCORE = 0 for challenges 43–48 and Bertrand "did no filtering according to existence for challenges 43–48" (i.e., participants knew these could not be retrieved) — casmi-contest.org/2014/results; 2014/results-cat2
  • 2016 design: Cat 2/3 compounds were authentic standards whose spectra were deliberately split so that spectra with MassBank similarity > 0.85 went to the training set; the authors recommended future contests "use true unknowns… provide open training sets that exclude challenge compounds" — PMC5368104; "Spectral similarity was used to split training and challenge sets" explains why MoNA library similarity added nothing in Cat 3 — PMC5368104

  • Historical de novo success is effectively 0 % at rank 1 (0/6 in 2014; 1/4 in 2017 only by the strongest fingerprint method, with the compound presumably being an analogue retrievable through near-neighbour chemistry).   • 2022 shows the ceiling for "structure in PubChem but no public spectra" under raw-data, single-answer conditions is ~25–30 % top-1 even for the field's best tool, which bounds expectations for 2026 class 2 before task-specific training on 2.5 M spectra.

  • The exact 2017 unknown structures and how many of 42–45 were "unreported NP" vs "synthetic analog" are only in the solutions page (not analysed here); the per-challenge ranks above are read from the results tables.
  • No CASMI edition reported a de novo generation baseline (e.g., MSNovelist appeared after 2017); the 2022 zero-score entries cannot be attributed to de novo methods without the methods excerpt.

Key Question 5 — What is explicitly inherited or changed in CASMI 2026 (Enveda revival), and which historical lessons transfer to the 2026 core challenges

Takeaway

CASMI 2026 inherits the name, the blind-test philosophy ("founded in 2012, inspired by CASP"), the natural-product focus of 2016/2017, and the connectivity-only evaluation (InChIKey block 1; now with explicit RDKit tautomer canonicalisation), with the original founders' "support" and an official page on casmi-contest.org; it changes almost everything operational — single fully-automatic category in a Kaggle code competition (offline notebooks), a rank-aware metric (MRR@25) instead of absolute-rank wins, hidden novelty-class stratification, a single vendor platform (Bruker timsTOF, 10 adducts) with no retention time, and a 2.5 M-spectrum curated training set replacing "bring your own data". Historical lessons that transfer most directly: candidate-database choice and popularity priors dominate class-2 outcomes but natural-product priors must replace reference-count priors; same-formula isomers are the residual error; consensus of heterogeneous scorers beats any single scorer; training/test structure overlap inflates scores (34 % → 18 %); and de novo has never scored.

Class 1 (structure has public spectra) Library search was the fastest, most accurate route whenever a reference spectrum existed (2012 Oberacher 3/3; 2016: 60 % of challenges solvable by library match alone; CSI:FingerID 70–95 % when the structure is in training) Those rates were measured with same-instrument or same-vendor libraries; 2026 test spectra are timsTOF while most of the 2.5 M training spectra are Orbitrap/QTOF (MSnLib, RIKEN, MoNA, GNPS) — cross-instrument similarity degradation was observed but never quantified in CASMI
Class 2 (in PubChem/COCONUT, no spectra) — candidate coverage + isomer discrimination Best spectrum-only top-1 ≈ 31–38 % (2016 Cat 2, 2017 Cat 2/4, SIRIUS 4 structure-disjoint); restricting the candidate DB to biomolecules nearly doubled it (37.8 % → 71.7 %); consensus of six tools beat each tool; negative mode weaker; "98 % same-formula isomer" errors match the historical residual (abietic acid, aminoanthraquinones, cytochalasins) Reference-count/data-source priors that produced 70 %+ in 2016 hurt on natural products in 2017 (top-5 59 % → 53 %) and no RT is available in 2026, so the 2016 Cat 3 recipe (RT + reference counts) is not reproducible; COCONUT/NP-likeness/taxonomy priors are the natural-product analogue
Class 3 (de novo, not in PubChem) Historical rank-1 success is zero (2014: 0/6; 2017: 1/4 only by the top method); the one near-success and participants' strategies were analogue-based (glucuronide of an aglycone; "structural analogs of known natural products"); organisers defined unknowns as modifications "that occur or could conceivably occur in nature" No CASMI edition evaluated generative de novo models, so there is no historical calibration for a 2026-era generator; the 2022 zero-score entries are not attributable
Instrument/chemistry shift (timsTOF test; synthetic drug-like Enveda-180 vs natural-product test) Every edition logged failures from acquisition artefacts: calibration (30 ppm, 2012), missing isotope patterns (2017), integer-mass library training (2016), inconsistent isotope ratios and instrument-to-instrument spectral differences (2016 Cat 1); 2017 Cat 2 winner explicitly re-tuned SIRIUS to Q-TOF settings and exploited MS^E isotope peaks Enveda-180 (synthetic, drug-like, timsTOF) is the only same-vendor training block; CASMI never tested a vendor-disjoint train/test split, so the size of this penalty must be estimated locally
Validation design 2016: train/test structure overlap inflated top-1 from 18 % to 34 % → structure-disjoint CV is mandatory; de Jonge: stratify by class, mass range, instrument; report top-1 as primary; 100-item subsets are noisy; SIRIUS-type methods degrade above 500 Da and are impractical above ~850 Da (2026 range 157–1,159 Da) Historical contests had no hidden class split; a 2026 local validation must additionally simulate the class 1/2/3 mixture and the adduct distribution (10 adducts vs 2–6 historically)
Metric (MRR@25) Top-1 vs Top-10 gaps were large (2017 Cat 2: 77 vs 147 of 243), so rank-aware credit matters; 2016 organisers warned results were "very sensitive to the method of declaring the winner" Historical absolute-rank/"gold" tallies and 2022 single-answer percentages are not directly convertible to MRR; only the published Top1/Top3/Top10 triples allow rough MRR bounds (e.g., 2017 Cat 4 CSI:FingerID: MRR ≥ 66/198 = 0.33 and ≤ (66 + 25/2 + 28/4)/198 ≈ 0.43)
Compute budget (Kaggle offline notebook) 2016 tool runtimes for 208 spectra: MetFragCL ~12 h, MAGMa+ 18 h, MS-FINDER ~1 day, CFM-ID ~2 days on a 48-CPU cluster; 2016 Cat 1 manual: ~5 h per compound Kaggle enforces a fixed runtime and no internet, excluding live PubChem/ChemSpider retrieval that every historical winner relied on; candidate databases must be pre-packaged
